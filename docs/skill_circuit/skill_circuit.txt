# CIRCUIT GRAPHICS — Procedural Rendering Rules

## 1. Objective

Generate visually coherent, realistic, modern Formula 1-style circuits using **100% procedural rendering**.

All circuit elements must be drawn directly by code using paths, polygons, curves, strokes, fills, gradients, noise and geometric primitives.

**Never use external assets, imported textures, sprites, raster images or AI-generated images.**

The rendering must remain deterministic, resolution-independent and consistent across all circuits.

These rules describe a unified artistic style inspired by modern European racing circuits. They are not intended to reproduce every FIA homologation requirement.

## 2. Core principles

1. **Geometry first:** derive every element from the actual track geometry.
2. **Track-relative placement:** position kerbs, runoff, barriers and markings relative to track edges, never using arbitrary screen coordinates.
3. **Consistent materials:** identical surfaces must share the same base palette.
4. **Context-aware rendering:** apexes, exits, chicanes and straights require different treatments.
5. **No arbitrary decorations:** do not invent colored markings, safety devices or obstacles without a meaningful reason.
6. **Top-down readability:** the road, track limits and obstacles must remain clearly distinguishable.
7. **Scalable rendering:** use world-space dimensions and convert them to pixels through the camera.
8. **Preserve existing geometry:** styling must not alter the track centerline, road width, corner radius or collision boundaries unless explicitly requested.

## 4. Track geometry and surface rendering

### 4.1 Main track

Render the road as a continuous polygon derived from the centerline and local track width.

- Base color: `#424448`.
- Avoid pure black.
- Apply subtle, low-contrast procedural grain.
- Add restrained asphalt variation using deterministic noise.
- Keep the road visually darker than the default runoff.
- Avoid visible polygon seams and abrupt changes in texture.
- Never render the track as disconnected rectangular segments.

If the engine supports material shaders, use world-space noise. Otherwise, use deterministic procedural geometry.

### 4.2 Asphalt wear and rubber

Rubber deposits should appear primarily along the racing line.

- Color: `#292A2C`.
- Use low opacity and soft transitions.
- Concentrate deposits near braking zones, apexes and acceleration zones.
- Avoid drawing a uniform black stripe around the entire circuit.
- Tire marks must follow physically plausible vehicle trajectories.

Rubber marks are decorative only and must never change the actual collision geometry.

### 4.3 Track boundaries

Both track edges must be computed from the centerline and the local normal.

Draw continuous white boundary lines:

- Color: `#F1F0E8`.
- Suggested visual width: 0.12–0.20 m.
- Use smooth joins.
- Do not interrupt lines arbitrarily.
- The white line must remain distinguishable from adjacent kerbs.

When kerbs are present, preserve the correct geometric relationship between the track edge, white line and kerb.

## 5. Kerbs

### 5.1 Default kerb style

Use alternating red and white segments.

- Red: `#C9282E`.
- White: `#F1F0E8`.
- Suggested width: 0.8–1.5 m.
- Suggested segment length: 0.6–1.2 m.
- Segments must follow the local track curvature.

Kerbs must be generated as curved strips following the edge geometry, not as independently rotated rectangles.

### 5.2 Kerb placement

Place kerbs primarily:

- On the inside of corners near apexes.
- On the outside of corners near exits.
- Along relevant chicane entry and exit sections.
- At other positions explicitly specified by circuit geometry or design metadata.

Do not place kerbs continuously along every straight or every track edge.

### 5.3 Kerb geometry

Kerbs must:

- Follow the exact local edge.
- Maintain approximately constant width.
- Use regular color alternation measured along arc length.
- Avoid overlapping neighboring sections.
- Remain smooth in tight curves.
- Maintain stripe continuity through sampling subdivisions.

Add only subtle relief or shading. In top-down view, avoid exaggerated 3D effects.

### 5.4 Apex-specific behavior

Do not invent special apex colors.

Use standard red/white kerbs unless a circuit-specific variant is configured.

The apex should be visually recognizable through the geometry and placement of the kerb.

### 5.5 Anti-cut kerbs

Raised yellow/orange anti-cut devices are optional and must be explicitly enabled.

- Yellow: `#E9BE37`.
- Orange: `#F17A27`.

Use them selectively at chicanes or other designated locations.

Never generate raised anti-cut devices automatically at every apex.

## 6. Runoff areas

### 6.1 Asphalt runoff

- Base: `#606267`.
- Must be visually distinguishable from racing asphalt.
- Must follow the geometry of the corner and available safety area.
- Use a coherent surface polygon, not a constant-width ring around the track.

Runoff areas are not necessarily symmetrical.

### 6.2 Painted runoff

Supported variants:

- Green: `#448C58`.
- Blue: `#2769A3`.
- Red: `#B83843`.

Painted areas must be configured by circuit style or segment metadata.

Do not apply Paul Ricard-style colored stripes to every circuit.

### 6.3 Gravel traps

- Base: `#C7B48C`.
- Use subtle procedural variation with `#A99874`.
- Generate irregular granular detail in world space.
- Avoid large repeated circular dots.
- Boundaries may be slightly irregular but must remain coherent.

Gravel traps should generally occupy appropriate exterior runoff zones, not arbitrary interior sections.

### 6.4 Grass

- Base: `#6D9652`.
- Dark: `#557944`.
- Dry: `#A2A06A`.

Use low-frequency color variation with restrained fine detail.

Grass must not look like a uniformly tiled pattern.

### 6.5 Surface transitions

Use sharp, well-defined boundaries between constructed surfaces:

- Asphalt to painted asphalt.
- Asphalt to concrete.
- Track to kerb.

Use more natural irregularity between:

- Grass and soil.
- Gravel and surrounding terrain.
- Vegetation and exposed earth.

Do not blur the physical boundary of the track.

## 7. Safety barriers

Safety barriers must follow dedicated safety boundaries, not automatically coincide with track edges.

### 7.1 Concrete walls

- Base: `#A9A9A3`.
- Light surfaces: `#D6D5CE`.
- Dark joints: `#81827E`.

Render as connected geometric modules with restrained joint lines.

### 7.2 Tire barriers

- Base: `#27292A`.
- Highlights: `#373A3C`.

Generate repeated circular or cylindrical tire geometry along the barrier path.

Tires must be individually recognizable at normal gameplay zoom without becoming visually noisy.

### 7.3 Metal barriers

- Base: `#A6AAAD`.
- Shadow: `#55585B`.

Draw parallel metal rails with regular support posts.

### 7.4 TecPro-style barriers

Supported colors:

- Red: `#C92F32`.
- White: `#E6E6DF`.
- Blue: `#28639C`.

Draw modular rectangular blocks following the barrier alignment.

### 7.5 Barrier placement

Barriers must be placed only when the circuit geometry or safety layout requires them.

Never surround the entire track with concrete walls by default.

Maintain reasonable separation between the road, runoff and barriers.

## 8. Bollards, cones and concrete blocks

Supported objects:

- Orange traffic cones.
- Yellow flexible bollards.
- White delineator posts.
- Grey concrete blocks.
- Red/white painted concrete blocks.
- Yellow/orange anti-cut elements.

Use procedural shapes only.

Every object must have:

- A defined world-space position.
- A defined orientation.
- A collision classification.
- A material color.
- A consistent scale.
- A small coherent shadow when appropriate.

Distinguish between flexible objects and solid obstacles.

Do not place decorative obstacles directly on the racing line.

## 9. Braking zones and corner entries

Do not paint artificial braking zones onto the asphalt.

Instead, use realistic visual cues:

- Distance boards.
- Rubber deposits.
- Trackside reference markers.
- Kerb geometry.
- Track width and corner shape.

Distance boards may display:

- 200 m.
- 150 m.
- 100 m.
- 50 m.

These distances must be measured along the track path to the relevant braking reference, not inferred from screen-space distance.

Do not generate all four boards if there is insufficient space or if the circuit configuration specifies otherwise.

## 10. Track markings

Supported markings:

- Continuous white track boundary lines.
- Start/finish line.
- Starting grid positions.
- Pit entry and exit lines.
- Pit box markings.
- Directional arrows where appropriate.
- Special race-control markings where configured.

All markings must be placed in world coordinates.

The start/finish line must be perpendicular to the local track tangent.

Grid positions must follow the local track direction and configured spacing.

Avoid unnecessary road markings typical of public roads.

## 11. Pit lane and paddock

Pit lane:

- Asphalt: `#4A4C50`.
- White markings: `#F1F0E8`.
- Concrete: `#A9A9A3`.

The pit lane must be a separate geometric road path, connected logically to the main track.

Draw:

- Pit entry.
- Pit exit.
- Pit lane boundaries.
- Pit boxes.
- Pit wall.
- Garage fronts when visible.
- Relevant signs and traffic lights.

Do not treat the pit lane as a decorative stripe alongside the circuit.

## 12. Trackside environment

Use procedural drawing for:

- Grass fields.
- Trees and bushes.
- Soil and gravel.
- Access roads.
- Service roads.
- Fences.
- Grandstands.
- Buildings.
- Camera towers.
- Signage.
- Advertising boards.
- Safety equipment.

The environment must remain visually subordinate to the track.

Avoid random placement of trees inside runoff areas, service roads or safety corridors.

All decorations must respect the circuit geometry and object exclusion zones.

## 13. Rendering order

Render elements in a consistent layer order, from back to front:

1. Base terrain.
2. Terrain variations.
3. Grass and soil.
4. Gravel traps.
5. Asphalt runoff.
6. Painted runoff.
7. Service roads.
8. Main track asphalt.
9. Pit lane.
10. Asphalt wear and rubber deposits.
11. Track boundary lines.
12. Kerbs and raised anti-cut elements.
13. Road markings.
14. Barriers and walls.
15. Bollards, cones and signs.
16. Buildings, vegetation and other trackside objects.
17. Vehicles and dynamic race objects.
18. Dynamic effects and gameplay overlays.

When layers intersect, use actual geometry and masks to prevent incorrect overpainting.

The precise order may be adjusted to account for local overlap, but the visual hierarchy must be preserved.

## 14. Geometry and procedural implementation

### 14.1 Coordinate system

Use world coordinates for all physical dimensions.

Screen coordinates must be derived from the camera transform.

Do not hardcode pixel distances for physical features.

### 14.2 Curve sampling

Sample curves adaptively according to curvature and zoom.

Requirements:

- Smooth track edges.
- Smooth kerb boundaries.
- No visible polygon gaps.
- No sharp artifacts at joins.
- Consistent stripe lengths along arc length.

### 14.3 Track-relative placement

Use:

- Centerline position.
- Tangent.
- Normal.
- Curvature.
- Local road width.
- Distance along the track.

Place objects relative to these quantities.

### 14.4 Procedural randomness

All random variations must use a deterministic seed.

The same circuit and seed must always produce the same appearance.

Do not regenerate random terrain detail on every frame.

### 14.5 Performance

Prefer:

- Cached geometry.
- Batched draw calls.
- Reusable procedural materials.
- Spatial culling.
- Level-of-detail adjustments.
- Precomputed static geometry.

Do not recalculate static track geometry every frame.

Caching generated geometry is allowed. Importing premade visual assets is not.

## 15. Circuit-specific configuration

The visual style must be configurable without changing rendering code.

Each circuit may define:

- Asphalt palette.
- Kerb palette.
- Kerb locations.
- Runoff materials and polygons.
- Gravel trap locations.
- Grass regions.
- Safety barrier paths and types.
- Pit lane geometry.
- Trackside objects.
- Painted runoff patterns.
- Braking boards.
- Special corner features.

Never infer exact safety infrastructure solely from track curvature.

When a circuit-specific feature is unknown, use the neutral default style rather than inventing a distinctive feature.

## 16. Validation rules

Before accepting a generated circuit, verify:

- [ ] The main track is continuous.
- [ ] Track widths are preserved.
- [ ] White boundary lines follow both track edges.
- [ ] Kerbs follow curved track boundaries.
- [ ] Kerbs appear at appropriate locations, not everywhere.
- [ ] Kerb stripe lengths are consistent.
- [ ] Apexes do not receive arbitrary special colors.
- [ ] Runoff regions do not overlap the main road.
- [ ] Gravel and grass regions are coherent.
- [ ] Barriers do not intersect the road unintentionally.
- [ ] Obstacles are not placed on the racing line.
- [ ] Pit entry and exit connect logically.
- [ ] Start/finish and grid markings follow the track geometry.
- [ ] All materials follow the shared palette.
- [ ] Procedural details are deterministic.
- [ ] Rendering is stable at different zoom levels.
- [ ] No external image or sprite assets are used.
- [ ] Styling does not silently modify driving physics.

## 17. Mandatory implementation behavior for AI coding agents

When implementing or modifying circuit rendering:

1. Inspect the existing rendering architecture before making changes.
2. Reuse the existing track geometry and coordinate system.
3. Separate track geometry, semantic circuit data and visual rendering.
4. Centralize the color palette and style parameters.
5. Implement procedural drawing rather than adding assets.
6. Preserve existing gameplay behavior and collision logic.
7. Avoid duplicating geometry calculations.
8. Never invent circuit-specific features without configuration data.
9. Validate the result at representative straights, tight corners, chicanes and pit lane sections.
10. If semantic information is missing, use a neutral fallback and report the missing data rather than pretending to know the correct placement.

**Priority order: geometric correctness → physical coherence → track readability → visual realism → decorative detail.**

The goal is a coherent racing circuit, not simply an attractive collection of colored polygons.
